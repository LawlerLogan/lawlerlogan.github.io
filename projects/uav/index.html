Custom UAV & Ground Station

Sub-250 g quadcopter designed, printed, wired, and tuned from scratch, with a Wi-Fi ground station that streams and logs live telemetry.

Design requirements

The goal was a quadcopter under 250 g, the weight class for small drones. That meant the lightest components that would still do the job, the shortest wire runs, and no frame material that wasn't carrying load.

Final takeoff weight came in at roughly 170 g on 3-inch propellers, about 80 g under the limit.

Airframe design

Designed in SOLIDWORKS around the 3-inch props, flight controller, receiver, GPS, and battery, then 3D printed. Design and manufacturing were the hardest parts of the project: everything had to be packed as tightly and as lightly as possible while keeping prop clearance and leaving the electronics reachable for service.

Each revision went through the same loop: model, print, test-fit the hardware, trim weight, repeat. Prototypes were printed in PLA, which is cheap and fast to iterate on, and the final frame in PAHT-CF, a carbon-fiber-filled nylon that is stiffer and holds up better to crashes.

Brass heat-set inserts are melted into the printed parts so the M2 screws thread into metal instead of plastic, which keeps the frame tight through repeated teardowns. A printed TPU strap holds the battery in place: flexible enough to stretch over for a quick swap, and grippy enough that the pack doesn't shift in flight.

Electronics & flight control

  • Flight controller running Betaflight, configured and tuned for the airframe
  • 1404-size, 3800 KV brushless motors on 3-inch tri-blade props
  • RP3 V2 ExpressLRS 2.4 GHz nano receiver for the control link from a RadioMaster transmitter
  • SEQURE M10-250 GPS module feeding position to the flight controller and the ground station
  • Power and signal wiring soldered by hand, routed to fit the compact frame

Ground station software

Telemetry rides the ExpressLRS link back down to the RadioMaster transmitter. The transmitter's built-in ExpressLRS Backpack, a small Wi-Fi radio inside the module, connects to the laptop's Wi-Fi and forwards that telemetry over the network as UDP packets. No extra telemetry radio is carried on the aircraft.

On the laptop, a Python ground station decodes the stream and serves it as a live, cockpit-style web dashboard, linked to an app that logs every flight for review afterwards. The dashboard has four parts:

  • Flight instruments: six analog gauges for groundspeed, attitude (an artificial horizon showing pitch and roll), relative altitude, throttle, heading, and climb rate
  • Live position: a map that tracks the aircraft, with distance and bearing back to home, plus GPS fix, satellite count, and HDOP
  • GNSS sky plot: where the GPS, GLONASS, Galileo, and BeiDou satellites should be overhead, predicted from their published orbital elements
  • Readouts: battery voltage, current draw, and capacity used and remaining; roll, pitch, and yaw; throttle and flight state

Result

A flying quadcopter at about 170 g, with every flight streamed live to the dashboard and saved to the log. The bigger outcome was two lessons from taking it from CAD to the air:

  • Design for printing, not just for CAD. A part that looks right on screen can still print weak or fit badly, so every revision was printed and fit-checked against the real motors, stack, and battery. Clearances change with material, since PLA, PETG-CF, and PAHT-CF each shrink differently. Filament condition matters too: nylon-based PAHT-CF absorbs moisture, and wet filament prints with bubbles and weak layer bonds unless it is dried first.
  • Integration is its own job. Each component working on the bench didn't mean the aircraft worked. Getting power, motors, flight controller, radio link, and GPS to run together meant chasing faults across the whole system, firmware included: the ExpressLRS transmitter, receiver, and Backpack all had to be flashed to matching versions before they would bind and pass telemetry, and Betaflight had to match its configurator.
