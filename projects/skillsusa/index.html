Competition brief

The 2025 SkillsUSA National Leadership & Skills Conference in Atlanta brought together state champions from 24 states. Competing as a two-person team from Utica CSI with Niken Patel, the task was to build a working automated inspection cell from scratch in about eight hours, with a one-hour break.

Every team got the same fenceless training cell: a Yaskawa Motoman GP4 six-axis robot, a Siemens LOGO! PLC and a set of sensors. The robot program did the inspecting and sorting, reading the limit switch and inductive sensor directly through the robot's I/O. The PLC, programmed in ladder logic, ran the stack light that showed the cell's status, and at the end handled the vision system. The sequence:

  • Pick each block from the slide and check all six faces for a hole, using a limit switch
  • No hole: place the block on the discard pile
  • Hole found: use an inductive sensor to tell metal from non-metal, then sort the block into the matching pile
  • Final requirement: replace the limit switch with a Cognex vision system that finds the hole, brought into the cell through the PLC

Random part loading

Blocks came down the slide in random order, so no drop point could be hard-coded. Each block still had to land in the next open spot of its pile.

Each pile's drop point was stored in a position register. The robot program kept a count of the blocks placed on each pile and incremented that pile's position register based on the count, so every block went to the next open spot no matter the loading order.

Machine vision integration

Neither teammate had used or programmed a machine-vision system before the competition. The Cognex camera was learned on the spot: setting up the image, teaching it what a hole looks like, tuning the inspection until it was reliable, and routing its pass/fail result through the PLC to the robot in place of the limit switch signal.

With no practiced routine to fall back on, it came down to reading the manual and testing in quick loops as the clock ran down, until the camera was reliably catching every hole.

Technical documentation

Alongside the working cell, each team turned in the documentation a technician would need to run and maintain it, written under the same eight-hour clock:

  • Flowcharts of the control logic, drawn with standard symbols: start and end points, process steps, decision points with yes and no branches (hole or no hole, metal or non-metal), and loops that repeat until the task is complete
  • Safety lists: the hazards in the cell and the steps for operating it safely
  • Work cell layouts: where the robot, slide, sensors, and sort piles sit relative to each other

Result

Gold medal and national champion. Both teammates were also offered FANUC certification tests at the conference, and both passed. The bigger outcome was what the day taught:

  • Design logic around state, not sequence. Random loading forced the cell to keep track of what it had already done instead of assuming a fixed order. Counting parts and incrementing position registers from that count is the same approach real production cells use, and it made the program shorter and easier to debug, not longer.
  • Learn new systems fast, under pressure. Integrating a vision system neither teammate had touched before, against the clock, came down to reading the documentation, testing in small steps, and wiring its output into logic that already worked. That habit of learning a new tool on demand carried straight into later projects.
