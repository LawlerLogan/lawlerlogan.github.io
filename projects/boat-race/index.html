Odysseus’s Ship Showdown Carnival Game

A life-size, two-player carnival race: every press moves a real boat down the track and its twin across the screen.

Players
Two: red vs. blue
Team
Four students
When
Oct 2024
Control
3 Arduino Uno R3s + a Godot game
Inputs
Button, capacitive hand pad, ultrasonic finish sensor
Build
2×4 & plywood frame, PLA printed parts
Skills
  • Mechanical & CAD
  • Software
  • Electronics
  • Fabrication

Gameplay

Two players stand at the front of the station, one on the blue lane and one on the red. Each has a hand-shaped conductive pad and a push button. Every press or touch pushes that player's miniature ship forward along a slot in their lane, and its twin forward on the TV above. First to the far end wins.

Electronics and controls

  • One Arduino per lane reads that player's button and capacitive touch hand pad, counts each press once, and reports it to the PC over USB serial. Arduinos were used in place of the Raspberry Pi's GPIO, which caused complications with the inputs
  • The game, built in Godot with C#, reads both serial ports and turns each player's presses per second into their ship's speed on screen
  • A third Arduino and an L298N H-bridge drive the two DC belt motors, with each motor's speed set by that player's press rate, so the real ship and its twin on the TV move together
  • An HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensor at the end of each lane sees the ship arrive and stops that lane counting; the first ship home wins
  • A PC ATX power supply provides 12 V for the motors and 5 V for the logic and 5 m of LED strip under the lanes
  • Sound plays through a speaker amplifier to the two speakers beside the TV

Mechanical design and build

Planned in hand sketches, then modeled in CAD as one standalone station with full drawings, including section and detail views of the belt drive and casters, and built full size:

  • A frame of 2×4s and plywood on casters, with gussets at the joints and a shelf underneath for the electronics
  • A play surface split into a blue lane and a red lane, each with a slot running its length
  • Under each lane, a belt loop runs between a drive pulley and an idler; the ship sits on a carriage that rides the slot, clipped to the belt (see section A–A and the details in the drawing below)
  • A backboard carrying the TV and two speakers
  • A front control strip with each player's hand pad and button
  • For the finished look, the lanes are dressed as a stormy sea with cotton waves and blue underlighting
  • Motor mounts, ships, and other components 3D printed in PLA

Result

The game was designed for third graders, so it had to be obvious at a glance and hold up to excited kids. That shaped the engineering as much as the look:

  • Simple, forgiving inputs. A big hand pad and a button per player. The Arduino counts a press only on the change from released to pressed, so holding a button down or leaning on the pad does nothing; only real presses move the ship
  • Speed from effort. Each lane measures presses per second and turns that rate into belt-motor speed and on-screen speed at the same time, so pressing faster visibly pays off on both the track and the TV
  • A clear winner. The ultrasonic sensor at the end of each lane stops that lane the moment its ship arrives, and the game declares the first ship home, so there are no arguments over a close finish
  • No setup between races. The station runs from a single ATX power supply and the game resets for the next pair of players

The finished station was a success with the third graders it was built for.
